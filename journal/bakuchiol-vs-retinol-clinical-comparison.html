Bakuchiol vs Retinol: A Clinical Comparison That Actually Uses Data

The internet is full of claims about bakuchiol being 'natural retinol'. Here is what the peer-reviewed research actually says — and why we chose it for the Luna Cream.

D

Dr. Camille Rousseau

Head of Formulation

Bakuchiol has been called everything from 'natural retinol' to 'retinol without the side effects'. The marketing claims run far ahead of the science. Let us look at what the peer-reviewed literature actually demonstrates.

What Retinol Does

Retinoids work by binding to nuclear retinoic acid receptors (RARs and RXRs), triggering gene expression changes that increase cell turnover, stimulate collagen synthesis, and suppress melanogenesis. This mechanism is well-established across 40+ years of clinical research.

The problem: retinoic acid (the active form all retinoids convert to) is also profoundly irritating. Retinaldehyde and retinol require conversion enzymes to reach the active form — a process that takes weeks and produces significant purging, peeling, and photosensitivity, particularly in sensitive or darker skin types.

What Bakuchiol Actually Does

A landmark 2018 study in the British Journal of Dermatology (Dhaliwal et al.) demonstrated that 0.5% Bakuchiol applied twice daily produced statistically equivalent reductions in wrinkle depth, pigmentation, and skin firmness vs 0.5% retinol over 12 weeks — with significantly fewer adverse events (burning, scaling, stinging).

Mechanistically, Bakuchiol does not bind to RAR receptors directly. Instead, it activates the same downstream gene expression pathways (collagen synthesis, MMP inhibition, cell turnover) through a different molecular route — one that does not trigger the same cascade of irritation.

The Caveat

Bakuchiol research is still relatively young. Retinol has 50 years of clinical literature; Bakuchiol has perhaps 10. Long-term effects at higher concentrations remain understudied. We use 1% Bakuchiol in Luna Cream — a concentration supported by current evidence, pending longer-term data.

For pregnancy-safe alternatives to retinol: Bakuchiol is currently considered the most evidence-backed option. Consult your physician for personalised advice.
